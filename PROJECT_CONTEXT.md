## Where the Idea Came From

We often look at a coffee shop and think about how many customers it has, how much coffee it sells, or how popular the brand is. But I’ve always found the business side more interesting.

What does it actually take to keep a coffee shop profitable? How much do everyday decisions matter? And can a business that seems to be doing well still struggle financially?

These questions are what led me to this project.

Having studied Economic Cybernetics, I wanted to work on something that connects economic thinking with data analysis and the tools we have available today.

Not just another dataset to explore, but a business situation where the numbers actually mean something.

## Why 5 to go?

I chose 5 to go because it’s a recognizable Romanian coffee shop brand built around a relatively simple concept: accessible coffee, quick service, and convenient locations.

What interests me is everything happening behind that concept.

A cup of coffee has a selling price, but it also comes with ingredient costs, packaging, employee time, rent, and other expenses.

And while selling more coffee sounds like a good thing, it doesn’t necessarily mean the business becomes more profitable.

I wanted to explore that difference.

## What I’m Trying to Understand

The main question behind this project is simple:

What actually makes a coffee shop profitable?

To explore it, I’ll look at several aspects of running a coffee shop:

- How revenue and operating costs interact.
- Why some products might be more profitable than others.
- How customer demand changes throughout the day.
- Whether past sales can help predict future demand.
- How changes in inventory, staffing, or pricing could affect financial performance.

I’m particularly interested in understanding how these factors influence one another, rather than analyzing each of them separately.

## My Approach

I’ll start by collecting publicly available information about 5 to go and relevant economic indicators from Romania.

Since internal sales and operational data aren’t publicly available, I’ll also work with simulated data representing a hypothetical coffee shop.

The idea isn’t to reproduce the actual financial performance of 5 to go, but to build a realistic enough business scenario to explore different economic decisions.

From there, I’ll use Python to analyze profitability, identify patterns in sales, and experiment with demand forecasting.

Eventually, I’d like to bring everything together in a small interactive application where different business scenarios can be explored.

## What I Hope to Get Out of This

I don’t expect to find one perfect formula for running a profitable coffee shop.

What I want is to understand how relatively small decisions can influence the bigger financial picture.

For me, the interesting part isn’t just building a model that predicts sales or creating a dashboard with attractive charts.

It’s being able to look at the results and ask:

So what does this actually mean for the business?

And, more importantly:

What could someone do differently based on what the data shows?

That’s the thinking I want to bring into this project.
